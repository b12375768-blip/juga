Attribution, Credits & "No Commercial Use"

Two words carry enormous weight in creator licensing and both are frequently misunderstood: credit and commercial. Getting them right is often the difference between a licence that holds and a claim that lands.

7 min read Licence literacy

Crediting an artist is a courtesy that satisfies licence terms and supports a good-faith argument. It is not a licence, it is not fair use, and it will not defeat a Content ID claim or a DMCA notice. Understanding why is the fastest way to stop losing revenue to well-intentioned mistakes.

Credit is not permission

Copyright law grants the owner the exclusive right to authorise copying. Permission comes from the owner — through a licence, a purchase, a platform programme, or a statutory exception. A credit line on your video is something you do, unilaterally, after the fact. It changes nothing about whether you had the right to copy.

The practical consequence: rights holders receive takedown notices routinely from videos that credit them perfectly. Credits are ignored in takedown review because they are not evidence of permission. The evidence is the licence document.

What credit actually does for you

  • It satisfies licence terms. Many libraries and artists require specific credit formats. Missing required credit is a licence breach even when you have permission.
  • It supports a fair use argument. Naming and crediting the source shows good faith and that you are not trying to pass the work off as your own — both relevant to the analysis.
  • It reduces market harm perception. Directing viewers to the original is friendlier to the rights holder.
  • It builds relationships. Artists who get credited are far more likely to say yes next time.
Rule of thumb

Credit is what you do because you have permission. Never as a substitute for it.

How to write a real credit line

Match the licence's requirements and be specific:

  • Standard music credit: "♫ [Track Title] by [Artist] — licensed via [Library] — [link]"
  • Direct permission credit: "♫ [Track Title] by [Artist], used with permission" (+ contact or link if they asked)
  • Public domain: "[Title] (public domain) — source: [archive]"
  • Stock footage: "Footage: [Clip Title] via [Library]"
  • CC-licensed work: "[Title] by [Creator], used under CC [BY / BY-SA / BY-NC] [link]" — and note that you complied with the licence's conditions.

Put credits in the video description (where platforms expect them) and, where the track is prominent, on screen at the start or end. If the licence required a specific format or a minimum display size, follow it exactly.

"Non-commercial use only" — what it actually restricts

This is the most consequential clause in creator licensing, because creators often add monetisation months after they made the video.

Using the track in…Non-commercial licence OK?
A video with no monetisation, on a personal channelUsually yes
A monetised video (ads before or during)No — commercial
A video with a sponsorship or paid promotionNo — commercial
A Patreon / paid-members-only postNo — commercial
A paid course, template pack, or preset sold to othersNo — commercial, and often a redistribution issue too
Ad revenue earned incidentally from an unmonetised uploadDepends on the exact wording; check the licence, and consider re-licensing
A client project (a video you make for a business)No — commercial; the client may also need their own licence
The retrofit problem

You made a video in year one with a non-commercial track. In year three you turn on monetisation and a sponsor reads it. The licence you relied on no longer covers what you are doing. This is very common, and the fix — replacing the track or upgrading the licence — is quick, but only if you check.

Creative Commons: the licence family

When a creator publishes under a Creative Commons licence, you get real, explicit rights. Know the conditions:

LicenceYou canYou mustYou cannot
CC BYUse and adapt for anything, including commerciallyCredit the creator, link the licence, note changes—
CC BY-SASame as BY, including commerciallyCredit, and release adaptations under the same licence—
CC BY-NCUse and adapt, non-commerciallyCreditAny commercial use
CC BY-NDUse as-is, including commerciallyCreditDistribute modified versions
CC BY-NC-NDUse as-is, non-commerciallyCreditCommercial use or modifications — the most restrictive CC option

A video published "CC BY-NC" on a channel you monetise is already outside what the creator permitted — the licence applies to your reuse of the video, and monetisation on the original channel is the creator's own choice. The point is to read the licence on the specific work you are reusing, not the one on your own upload.

Attribution best practice for creators

  • Credit every third-party element, not just the music — footage, images, fonts, templates, sound effects.
  • Follow the exact format the licence requires; do not paraphrase it.
  • Link to the source and to the licence where you can.
  • Name the people behind the work, not just the brand or the account.
  • Where the licence required it, get written permission for the credit before you publish.
  • Keep your credits in a reusable text snippet so you never have to reconstruct them under deadline.
  • Re-check credits when you re-upload a video to a new platform — platforms may strip or truncate descriptions.

Summary

Credit generously — it costs nothing and it matters. But never confuse it with permission, and treat "non-commercial" as a live constraint you have to re-check every time a video becomes more commercial than it was when you made it.
