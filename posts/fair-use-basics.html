Guide 7 · Fair use

Fair Use Basics

Fair use is the legal exception that lets you use copyrighted work without permission. It is not a length limit, not a checkbox, and not a defence you can claim after the fact. This is how it actually works.

11 min read The most important guide here

Fair use is a balancing test. Courts weigh four factors, no single one decides the case, and the strength of your purpose does most of the work. Understanding that one idea is worth more than memorising any rule of thumb.

What fair use actually is

Copyright law reserves certain uses to the public interest: criticism, comment, news reporting, teaching, scholarship, research. In the US this is the four-factor test of section 107. Many other countries use similar "fair dealing" lists — often narrower, with fewer permitted purposes.

Crucially: fair use is a defence, not a permission. You are not exempt because you declared it. If the rights holder sues, you have to prove the use was fair — and the burden is on you.

Platforms are not courts

On YouTube, your fair use dispute is decided by the platform's staff and influenced by the rights holder, not by a judge. A genuine fair use argument can still be overruled by a platform's automated systems or by a counter-notice. Treat fair use as your best case argument, not as a safe harbour.

The four factors

1. Purpose and character of use

The most important factor, and the one creators most often misread. Courts look at:

  • Transformative? Does your use add a new purpose, meaning, message or perspective — or is it a substitute for the original?
  • Commercial? Monetised videos are commercial, but commercial use is not automatically unfair. A monetised review can be fair; a monetised repost of someone else's video is not.
  • Good faith? Believing your use is legitimate counts for something.
  • Bad faith? Takedown-tool abuse, lying about the source, and repeated infringement after warnings all count against you.

2. Nature of the copyrighted work

Published versus unpublished (unpublished works get stronger protection). Factual versus creative — facts and news lean toward fair use; novels, films, songs and artwork lean away. Non-commercial and educational uses lean toward fair use.

3. Amount and substantiality used

How much, and how much of the heart of the work. A percentage alone is not decisive: taking 100% of a 3-second clip can be fair, and taking 15% of a song can be unfair if you took the chorus. Qualitative substantiality — is this the most recognisable, valuable part? — matters.

4. Effect on the potential market

The factor most creators ignore and the one that decides a lot of cases. Would this use substitute for the original? Would it harm the market for the licensed version? Note that this factor considers harm from fair uses as well — courts recognise that allowing transformative criticism to cannibalise sales is part of the bargain the law makes.

Why the factors exist

Copyright is not an absolute monopoly. The factors are the mechanism for balancing the copyright owner's interest against the public's interest in new expression, scholarship and commentary. Read with that in mind, a strong transformative purpose usually does most of the heavy lifting — and a purely substitutive use usually loses regardless of length.

What fair use is NOT

  • Not a time limit. There is no "10 seconds is fine" rule.
  • Not a percentage. Taking 5% can be unfair; taking 100% of a tiny work can be fair.
  • Not granted by credit. Attribution does not create fair use.
  • Not a free pass for entertainment. "I just want people to watch it" is not transformative.
  • Not a strategy you apply after the fact. Building your channel around others' content and adding commentary later is the weakest possible version of the argument.

Making your fair use case stronger

  • State the purpose up front. Say out loud, and in the description, that the video is a critique / review / parody of the source.
  • Use the minimum necessary. Cutting from 45 seconds to 12 seconds is the single biggest improvement you can make.
  • Add substantial original contribution. Analysis, structure, argument, humour, framing. Add your own voice throughout, not only at the start.
  • Avoid substituting for the original. Do not let the clip carry the segment; let your commentary carry it.
  • Name and credit the source so viewers can find the original — this reduces market harm in the eyes of the analysis.
  • Keep your argument on record. Note the purpose, the specific clips and why each was needed, before you upload.
  • Accept the possibility of a dispute. Build fair use into work you could afford to have removed, not into your revenue backbone.

Your decision checklist

Before publishing any borrowed material, answer these in writing:

  1. What is my purpose — criticism, comment, review, parody, teaching, or something else?
  2. Am I adding a new meaning, message or perspective, or am I repackaging?
  3. How much did I take, and why is that amount necessary for my purpose?
  4. Am I using the most memorable or valuable part of the work? Could a different segment work?
  5. Could a viewer watch my video instead of the original and lose something meaningful?
  6. Am I commercial, and does that change the risk?
  7. If disputed, could I write three sentences justifying each excerpt?

If you cannot answer question 1 with one of the recognised purposes, fair use is not your route — go and get a licence. See Music Licensing Basics and Asking Artists for Permission.

Summary

Fair use protects new expression built on old work. It rewards a genuine critical, educational or parodic purpose, minimal necessary excerpts, and low market harm. It does not reward length limits, attribution, or entertainment value. Purpose first, then amount — everything else follows from that.
