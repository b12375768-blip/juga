Royalty-Free, Copyright-Free & Public Domain

These three terms are used interchangeably in YouTube titles and they mean completely different things. Knowing the difference tells you exactly what you're allowed to do before you press upload.

7 min read Music safety

"Free" in music marketing almost never means "unrestricted". It usually means one of three quite different things, and mixing them up is how creators end up monetisation-blocked or claimed.

Quick definitions

TermWhat it meansDo you need to do anything?
Public domainThe copyright has expired, was never renewed, or was dedicated by the owner. No permission exists or is needed — the work is free for anyone to use.Nothing. Attribution optional but appreciated.
Royalty-freeThe copyright is still very much alive. You pay a fee (or a subscription) and the creator waives ongoing royalties. Use is conditional on the licence.Read and follow the licence. Often credit is required.
Copyright-freeMarketing term with no fixed legal meaning. Usually means "you don't have to pay ongoing royalties" — i.e. it is a synonym for royalty-free.Treat it as royalty-free until proven otherwise.

Public domain: free, but with two traps

Trap one: the recording is still protected. A melody from 1910 may be public domain, but the 1994 studio recording of it is a separate, protected sound recording. Use the recording and you need a licence for the recording. This bites people most with classical and jazz catalogues, where modern masters of old compositions are commercially valuable.

Trap two: restorations are new works. A film from 1923 might be public domain, but a 4K restoration with a newly recorded score, new intertitles or a colourised version is a copyrighted derivative. Use an original scan if you want to be safe.

Verifying public domain status

The US Copyright Office maintains a Public Records system for records, renewed registrations and post-termination notices — a renewal notice means the work is not public domain. Outside the US, look at your national library or collecting society. When in doubt, prefer works you can trace to a clear public domain source such as an archive with a dedicated public domain collection.

Royalty-free: the licence is the whole story

Royalty-free music is not free of conditions. It means the creator is not asking for a cut of your revenue every time the track plays. In exchange you accept limits. The clauses that actually catch creators out:

  • "Non-commercial use only" — no monetisation, no ads, no sponsorship reads, no paid products. Many free music libraries default to this.
  • "No redistribution / cannot be used in other projects" — you cannot offer the track as part of a template pack, a course, or another creator's video.
  • "Attribution required" — must be credited in a specific format, sometimes with a link. Miss it and you breached the licence.
  • "Single project / single end use" — one video only, not a series. Common with per-track purchases.
  • "Cannot be used in paid advertising" — organic posts yes, ad campaigns no.
  • "Content ID may be enabled" — even "royalty-free" tracks get fingerprinted by their creators, which is exactly how other channels get claimed by an "independent" artist.
The Content ID trap

The most common surprise for new creators: a track downloaded from a free library ends up claimed anyway, because the person who uploaded it to that library did not own the rights, or because the artist later enrolled the track in Content ID. Free downloads are not rights-cleared by default. If a "royalty-free" track appears on a Content ID reference track, treat the claim on its merits — see Content ID Claims Explained.

A quick decision tree

  1. Is it public domain? Check the copyright term for the work, and separately check the term for any modern recording or restoration. If both are expired → fine to use, no conditions.
  2. Did you pay a licence from a reputable library or platform? If yes → check that the licence covers your platform, monetisation, term and any "perpetual" requirement. Save it.
  3. Did an artist give you written permission? If yes → check it names platforms, monetisation and term. Ambiguous permission gets ambiguous claims.
  4. None of the above? Do not use it. A track you found in another creator's video, a ripped CD, a "no copyright music" compilation, or a random download is unlicensed. Find a properly licensed alternative.

Practical habit

When you adopt a track, drop a line in your project notes: track name, source URL, licence type, licence file location, platforms cleared, and date. When you switch libraries or your channel grows, re-check the handful of tracks you lean on most — subscriptions expire, and a track cleared in year one on a hobby channel may need fresh clearance for a brand deal in year three.
