Copyright Basics for Creators

Everything else on this site assumes you understand five things. This is the page that makes the rest click — what is protected, who owns it, and what a licence actually gives you.

8 min read Beginner No jargon

Copyright is not a rule about how long you can use something. It is a bundle of specific rights, owned by specific people, that you either have permission to exercise or you do not. Once you see it as a bundle rather than a blanket ban, most creator problems become simple: figure out which rights matter, then go get those permissions.

What copyright actually covers

Copyright protects original expression — not ideas, facts, styles, genres or techniques. Copyrighted works include:

  • Music — the composition (melody, lyrics, chord structure) and the sound recording (the actual performance) are two separate copyrights.
  • Films and TV — the footage itself, plus the soundtrack, plus sometimes the screenplay.
  • Photography and artwork — including AI-adjacent work where a human made meaningful creative choices.
  • Video games — code, art, music and level design.
  • Writing — articles, books, scripts, lyrics, subtitles.
  • Architecture and designs — in many countries, buildings are protected too.

What copyright does not cover: facts, the idea of a tutorial ("how to tie a bowline knot"), genre conventions ("this is a true crime podcast"), short phrases, and your own original expression.

The two-copyrights trap

One song in your video can involve four different rights holders: the songwriter (composition), the performer and producer (recording), and sometimes the label and the publisher. A film clip adds the studio and sometimes the network. This is why "I found the artist and asked them" is sometimes not enough — see Music Licensing Basics.

Copyright is automatic — nobody has to register it

Protection begins the moment the work is created and fixed in some form. There is usually no copyright symbol required and no registration required for protection to exist in most countries.

Registration and recordation matter for a different reason: enforcement. In the United States, registering with the Copyright Office before infringement (or within three months of publication) unlocks statutory damages and attorney fees. In many countries, a registered or deposited work is dramatically easier to enforce. That is a practical advantage for the owner, not a condition of protection.

Who owns it: work for hire, assignments, and the creator's own work

Ownership follows the employment contract, not who pressed record or who typed the code. Three rules cover almost everything:

SituationWho owns the copyrightWhat to check
You make something in the course of your job or courseUsually your employer, if it's a work made for hire under the contract or local lawYour contract's IP clause. Freelance work is often not work for hire.
You commission a freelancerYou, but only if the contract assigns the copyright to you in writingThe signed agreement. Paying someone does not transfer copyright automatically.
You collaborate with someoneShared, unless the contract says otherwiseWho owns the master and who gets separate permission.
You make it purely on your own time and own gearYouNothing — but document it anyway with dated files.
You use an AI toolVaries, unresolved in many countriesThe tool's terms, plus whether a human contributed protectable expression.

Moral rights are a related concept in many countries: the right to be named as author, and to object to distortion of the work. These rarely stop a fair commercial licence, but they do mean you should not crediting someone else's music as your own original composition.

What a licence actually gives you

A licence is permission, not ownership. The owner keeps the copyright and keeps every right the licence did not grant. A good licence answers six questions, and you should be able to answer all six for every piece of media in your project:

  1. What work? — which track, which recording, which version.
  2. Which rights? — sync (picture + sound together), mechanical (reproduce the composition), performance, and so on.
  3. Where? — YouTube only? All platforms? TV? In-store?
  4. How long? — in perpetuity, or for 3 years?
  5. Which territory? — worldwide, or one country?
  6. Commercial or not? — can it run in a monetised video, behind an ad, or in a paid course?
"Non-commercial only" is the most common trap

A track licensed to you for "personal, non-commercial use" is not cleared for a monetised video, a sponsorship read, a Patreon-exclusive episode, or a paid template. Monetisation can be switched on later, years later, and that is when a "harmless" track becomes a revenue problem.

The four permissions that solve most problems

Almost every creator copyright question reduces to one of these. Learn them and you can self-diagnose most situations before you publish:

1. Public domain

The copyright has expired, was never renewed, or was dedicated to the public. Free for anyone to use, no permission needed. Attribution appreciated, not required. Be careful with recordings of public domain music — see below.

2. Licensed stock

You bought a licence from a stock library (music, footage, images, templates). The licence terms define what is allowed. Read them; they are the permission.

3. Direct permission

You wrote to the rights holder and got written permission, ideally a licence agreement. Keep it. This is the route for indie artists, podcasts and interviews. See Asking Artists for Permission.

4. A statutory exception

Law lets you use the work without permission for specific purposes: fair use / fair dealing, quotation, criticism, review, parody, education, private study. Fair use is the one creators most often misapply — read Fair Use Basics before relying on it.

Public domain is not the same as "free to use any way"

A very old song's composition may be public domain while every modern recording of it is still protected. If you take the audio from a 2010 recording of a 1900 melody, you are using a protected recording. The same trap applies to films: a 1920s film may be public domain, but a restored 2015 print with new score and new intertitles is a new copyrighted work.

In music specifically, the composition became public domain in most countries around 70–100 years after the author's death, but the recording usually needs a separate term to run out. Check both.

Attribution is courtesy, not permission

Crediting an artist does not create a licence, and a credit line will not defeat a Content ID claim or a DMCA notice. What credit does do is make you a better neighbour, satisfies licences that require it, and gives you a record of where the media came from. Do it anyway — it just is not the thing that makes you legal. There is more in Attribution, Credits & "No Commercial Use".

Your own work can be claimed too

Creators get Content ID claims on their own channels through two common causes: using a distributor who registered music you already had out elsewhere, and pressing/mixing two tracks that both contain a registered sample. Turn on Content ID for music you own, keep proof of first publication with dated files and stems, and register your work with distribution platforms early.

Where to go next

You now know what the rules are. In order: licence the music properly, learn what fair use really covers, get movie and TV clips right, and then know how to fight a claim before you need to.
