DMCA Takedowns & Copyright Strikes

This is the serious one. A DMCA notice is a legal allegation, not a platform algorithm — and it can remove a video or end a channel. Here is what is happening, and exactly what to do next.

9 min read Urgent · Read now

A takedown notice tells the platform that your video infringes someone's copyright. In most jurisdictions the platform must act on it quickly — often within days — without first determining whether it is valid. Your job is to respond correctly and promptly. Most people who lose their channels did so by doing nothing.

What a DMCA notice does

  1. The notice is sent. A rights holder submits a takedown notice to the platform, usually through an agent or automated service. It names the work, your video, and asserts ownership.
  2. The platform removes the video (or restricts it). Under anti-circumvention rules such as the US DMCA, platforms that host user content are required to act on valid notices quickly. This is called "notice and takedown" — the platform removes first, and the merits are considered later, if at all.
  3. You are notified. Usually by email: the video is removed or blocked, and you may respond.
  4. You respond. Either with a counter-notice (if you believe the removal was wrong) or by licensing the material retroactively (if you had permission but no record of it).
  5. The claimant may sue. If you file a counter-notice, the rights holder has a limited window to bring a court action. If they do, liability for damages can shift to you — so counter-notices are taken seriously and should be accurate.
Do not ignore it

The single most damaging thing you can do is nothing. Platforms treat abandonment as an admission in policy terms, and repeated unresolved notices accumulate as strikes. Respond within the deadline, even if your response is simply "I am contacting the rights holder to license this."

Strikes: the accumulation rule

On most major video platforms, copyright strikes accumulate against a channel. The widely implemented model is:

  • First strike — warning; the video is removed; features like live streaming may be restricted.
  • Second strike — further restrictions on uploads and features.
  • Third strike — channel termination.
  • Counter-strike on appeal — if your appeal is upheld, the strike is removed and you may gain back some status.

The usual expiry period for a first strike is around 90 days (platform-specific and subject to change). Your dashboard is the authority on your channel — check it rather than relying on any general rule.

A rights holder can also submit "fraudulent/DMCA" notices against channels that repeatedly upload infringing content, as a tool to terminate channels faster. This is why repeated infringement and repeated failed disputes have consequences well beyond any single video.

Responding correctly: your options

Option A — you have a licence, and you can prove it

  • Gather the licence document, receipt, or the permission email thread with dates.
  • File a counter-notice stating the video was used under licence, and attach the evidence.
  • Be accurate and factual. Do not speculate about the claimant's motives in the notice.
  • If the platform upholds the removal but the claimant does not sue, the video is restored through the platform's appeal process.

Option B — your use was fair use

  • Counter-notice, stating the use is fair use under the applicable exception.
  • Explain the purpose (criticism, comment, parody, teaching) and why it is transformative and necessary.
  • Be aware you are now putting a reasoned legal position in writing that you may need to stand behind in court. If the matter is serious, get advice.

Option C — you did not have permission

  • Do not counter-notice. You would be making a false statement under penalty of perjury.
  • Remove the video, or edit to remove the infringing material, if the rest of the work still has value.
  • Contact the rights holder directly and ask to license retroactively — many will, especially for older videos and small creators.
  • Take the strike. One strike and a genuine fix is survivable; a pattern is not.

The counter-notice, in plain terms

A counter-notice is a formal, sworn statement that the material was removed through mistake or misidentification. It typically must include:

  1. Your physical or electronic signature.
  2. Identification of the material that was removed (the video URL).
  3. Your name, address and contact information.
  4. A statement, under penalty of perjury, that you have a good-faith belief the material was removed as a result of mistake or misidentification.
  5. A statement, under penalty of perjury, that you consent to the jurisdiction of the federal district court for the district in which you reside (or, if outside the US, any district in which the platform may be served), and that you will accept service of process from the person who filed the notice.

That last point is why counter-notices are not something to file casually. File one only when you are confident it is accurate.

If the takedown is wrong or abusive

Sometimes notices are sent in error, by an automated service, or by someone who does not actually own the rights (common in music, where several parties claim the same track). Options:

  • Contact the notifier directly with your licence evidence — many disputes are resolved this way without any legal filing.
  • Check whether the claimant is an agent for the actual owner; agents often have authority to withdraw notices.
  • Look for a specific works list. Some automated services send notices for content outside their mandate; a precise challenge citing that is effective.
  • For repeated or fraudulent notices, platforms have abuse-reporting channels for rights holders.
Retaliation is counter-productive and can be unlawful

Threatening a rights holder, doxxing them, or coordinating harassment campaigns against them is a bad idea regardless of whether you think they were wrong. Keep the dispute factual and on the platform's process. Platforms remove channels for this behaviour faster than for copyright disputes.

Protecting your channel going forward

  • Keep a licence log for every video: music, footage, images, where the licence came from, date, and scope.
  • Do not use music you cannot trace to a licence or a permission thread.
  • Separate videos you cannot fully clear — put them on a backup channel or keep them unlisted with no promotion.
  • Respond to every claim and notice within the deadline, even if the response is short.
  • Do not repeatedly re-upload the same content after a strike; it triggers fraud notices.
  • Register your own work and enable Content ID, so your rights are enforced and misattribution is traceable to the source.

Summary

A takedown is urgent and procedural. Respond fast. If you had permission, prove it. If you relied on fair use, argue it with reasons. If you had neither, fix it and license retroactively. Three strikes is the number everyone remembers — but the number that matters is how many you let accumulate by not responding.
