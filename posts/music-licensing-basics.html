Music Licensing Basics

Every video with a soundtrack needs a licence. The confusing part is that one song can need two different permissions from two different people. Here is how the system actually fits together.

10 min read YouTubers & streamers Includes templates

A song is not one thing. There is the composition (the notes and words, written by a songwriter) and the recording (the particular performance you are hearing, owned by whoever produced it). Using them in a video touches both. Understanding that split is the fastest way to get unblocked.

Two copyrights, one song

Imagine a song you love. It was written by Ava Reyes, performed by The Lantern Club, produced by a label, and released by a publisher. Now you want to use 20 seconds of it as background music in a monetised YouTube video. There are two rights you need:

Master (sound recording)The specific audio you are playingPerformer and/or record labelYou are reproducing and distributing that exact recording.

PerformancePublicly playing the workPRO / collecting societyUsually handled by the platform or broadcaster, not you directly.

In practice, indie creators usually only ever chase the sync + master pair, and often get both from one person — the artist or their label.

The five situations, and what each one needs

1. Stock / library music

You buy a track from a library and its own licence applies. Read it. Check: which platforms, monetisation allowed, perpetual or term, client/transferable to a client, and whether you can use it in paid ads. Save the licence PDF with the project. This is the fastest, safest route for regular uploaders.

2. Music from a content library you already have

YouTube and Facebook offer music libraries whose licences are part of the platform. They are convenient and safe on that platform — but the tracks are often only cleared for use within that platform. Export the same video to your Instagram, TikTok, or embed it on your website and you may be infringing. When in doubt, check whether the track is cleared "across platforms" before you rely on it.

3. Independent artist reached out directly

The most common and most misunderstood case. You email an artist, they say "yeah go for it", you use the song. Is that a licence? A casual "go for it" can be, but it is fragile: it usually does not specify platforms, term, territory, monetisation or exclusivity. Ask for a short written licence — see Asking Artists for Permission.

4. Record labels and major publishers

Harder. Many labels have blanket sync licensing via agencies (for example, publishing admin companies offering sync on their catalogue). If a track does not have a listed contact, the realistic options are to use a music service, or to not use the track. Spending weeks emailing a label's publicity address is a common dead end.

5. Cover songs

Playing someone else's song yourself is a different world: mechanical licensing, not sync. There is usually a route through your distributor or your country's collecting society for royalties, but you do not get the master recording, and some songs (notably those controlled by one publisher or by major labels) have no available mechanical licence at all. Check before you record.

What to check before you publish

  • Do I have the sync right — i.e. permission to pair this music with video?
  • Do I have the master right — i.e. permission to use this exact recording?
  • Does the permission cover monetisation (ads, sponsorships, paid memberships)?
  • Does it cover every platform I actually publish on, plus embeds on my own site?
  • Is the permitted duration enough for what I planned?
  • Does it need credit, and in what form?
  • Is it exclusive in any way? (Rare but check — some licences forbid using other music in the same project.)
  • Is the permission in writing and saved where I can find it later?
Keep a one-line licence note per project

In your project notes, write: track name — who granted permission — date — where it is cleared for — link to the licence. When a claim arrives six months later, this single line is the difference between a 20-minute dispute and a week of panic.

Common mistakes

  • Using a track's "free download" version. A free download is a giveaway, not a giveaway of rights. Read what it actually licenses.
  • Ripping a track from a compilation. The uploader had no rights to give you. Ask the actual rights holder.
  • Assuming "credit = permission". It does not.
  • Using a track on a private/unlisted video then making it public. Fair use arguments and licences can differ by audience; an unlisted link still reaches an audience.
  • Adding the track back to old videos later. If a video is now monetised or your channel is bigger, re-check what you actually cleared.

Summary

Song = two copyrights. Video + song = sync. Video + that specific audio = master. Get both, in writing, with platforms / monetisation / term spelled out. Keep the proof. That is the whole job.
