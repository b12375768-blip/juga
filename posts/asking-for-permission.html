Asking Artists for Permission

Direct licensing is the best route in creator work: cheap, fast, and it builds a real relationship with the person whose work you depend on. It only works if your request is short, specific and easy to say yes to.

6 min read Copy-paste templates

Most licence requests fail for boring reasons: too long, too vague, or asking for something the artist cannot legally grant. A good request is under 150 words, names the exact track, the exact platforms and the exact money, and makes a single clear ask.

Before you write: work out who actually owns it

Check whether the artist is independent or signed to a label. Independent artists can usually grant a licence themselves for both composition and master. Signed artists often cannot — their label controls sync, and asking them to grant it wastes everyone's time and can read as presumptuous.

  • Signed to a label? Look for the label's sync or licensing contact, or use a library that already handles it.
  • Published by a third party? The publisher may hold sync rights even for an independent artist.
  • Not sure? Ask the artist directly: "Are you independent, or do you have a label/publisher I should go through?" Most artists will tell you.

The template that gets replies

Subject: Licence request — "[Track Title]" for a YouTube video

Hi [artist name],

I'm an independent creator and I'm putting together a video about [topic]. I'd love to use "[Track Title]" as the background music for it.

Here's exactly what I'm asking for, so you can say yes or no without any back-and-forth:

  • Track: [Track Title] (the [album/EP] version)
  • Use: approximately [X] seconds as background music in a [length] video about [topic]
  • Platforms: YouTube, Instagram, TikTok, and embedded on my website
  • Commercial: Yes — the video will be monetised / sponsored
  • Term: Perpetual, or for [X] years
  • Credit: I'll credit you as "[Artist] — [Track Title]" in the description and on-screen
  • Offer: I'm happy to pay $[X], or to credit you prominently, or both — whichever suits you

If you're open to it, could you reply with a short written confirmation covering the points above? I'm happy to sign a simple licence agreement if you have a standard one.

Thanks for your time — and if it's not a fit, no problem at all, I'll find something else.

[Your name]
[Channel link]
[Optional: audience size, typical views, why their music fits]

Follow-ups and etiquette

  1. Send once, then wait. Seven to ten days. Artists are busy. No reminder in week one.
  2. One polite follow-up. "Hi [name], just bumping this in case it got buried — happy to use a different track if it's easier."
  3. Never argue with a "no". Thank them, use something else. Arguing with an artist about their own song is a bad look and the internet is small.
  4. Report properly if they say no. Check the video before you post, and remove or mute the track.
  5. Send the finished video. Creators who share the final result when it goes live become the artists who say yes next time. This is the whole flywheel.

How much to offer

There is no going rate. For a small channel, a meaningful approach is: an honest fee in the low-to-mid three figures for a monetised video with a permanent term, or $0 plus prominent credit and a share of the video's reach. Offer what you can genuinely pay, be transparent about your audience, and be willing to negotiate. Many indie artists will take credit over cash; many will take cash over credit.

Say no to exclusivity unless you need it

Some artists offer exclusive licences in exchange for a bigger fee. Exclusivity means you cannot use other music in the same project, and you cannot use this track anywhere else — a serious constraint for creators. Only accept it for a clearly better outcome, and get the exclusivity scope in writing.

Recording the permission so it holds up

A DMY email thread is evidence, but it is fragile. Convert it into something durable:

  • Screenshot the full thread including headers and dates.
  • Save the audio file or download receipt of the version you actually used.
  • Write a licence note: track, rights granted, platforms, term, date, contact, and where the thread is stored.
  • If you are a business, put the licence in a signed PDF with your company details.
  • If you have repeated use of the same track across videos, ask for a standing licence covering future videos up front.

If you are ever disputed, this documentation is exactly what the platform's dispute process asks for. It also protects you if the artist later enrols the track in Content ID — you have a documented licence, and disputes with evidence attached succeed far more often. See Content ID Claims Explained.

When you cannot get permission

  • The artist doesn't respond. Silence is not permission. Use something else.
  • The artist says yes but the label disagrees. Ask the artist to confirm in writing that they control both composition and master — or abandon the track.
  • Rights holder is unreachable. Switch to a properly licensed track. Do not use it "temporarily".

Summary

Be specific, be brief, be reasonable, ask one clear question. Get it in writing, convert it to a saved record, and credit visibly. This is the most reliable licence route available to an independent creator — and it is how long-term music partnerships start.
