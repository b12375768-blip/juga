Stock Footage, Images & Templates

Stock media is the cheapest copyright insurance available — if you actually read the licence. Most stock problems are not "I got caught using it illegally", they are "I bought the wrong type of licence for what I was doing".

8 min read Editors & designers

Every stock asset comes with a licence. That licence is a contract that defines exactly what you may do, where, for how long, and whether you can pass that right on to a client. Ten minutes of reading prevents every realistic problem in this guide.

What "royalty-free" stock actually means

You pay once (or subscribe) and owe no further royalties. You do not own the asset, and you do not have unlimited freedom to use it. The licence grants a bundle of permissions, and the bundle has edges.

The licence types you will meet

The standard-licence resale trap

A very common and very expensive mistake: buying a clip on a standard licence, dropping it into a template, and selling the template. Standard licences typically prohibit that. The buyer of your template does not have rights to the underlying footage either, so you may be both infringing and shipping a product you cannot warrant. Buy an extended or template licence explicitly.

Model releases and property releases

Stock clips showing recognisable people, private property, artworks or trademarks often come in three flavours:

  • No release — a stranger in a crowd. Fine for background use; risky for anything that looks like endorsement.
  • Model release — the person signed permission for commercial use of their likeness. Check whether the release covers your territory and term.
  • Property release — permission from the owner of a private building, a visible artwork, or a branded product.

If you use a clip without the right release in a commercial context, you have a second problem beyond copyright: the subject may have a claim against you. This is why "editorial use only" clips should stay in genuinely editorial videos.

Footage of other people's content, inside stock footage

A clip of Times Square will contain billboards. A clip of a stadium may contain broadcast footage. A clip filmed at a concert contains other people's music and performances. Check whether stock libraries state "no recognizable third-party content" and whether the clip is flagged as containing trademarked or copyrighted material. Rarely is that enough to be fully safe, but it tells you where the risk sits.

Music, sound effects and AI-generated assets

  • Music bundled with stock footage — a "royalty-free" bed in a clip is usually licensed to you under the same library terms. Check whether it covers monetised content on third-party platforms, and whether it can be extracted.
  • Sound effects — cheaper and safer than music, but check for "not for use in commercial productions" wording.
  • AI-generated images and video — licence quality varies wildly. Some licences prohibit use where the output competes with the tool, others demand disclosure. Avoid prompts that name living artists or request a specific artist's style in your marketing, and check the tool's current terms.

A licence record worth keeping

For each asset, save: asset ID, library, licence type, download date, licence PDF or receipt, and the end products you used it in. A shared folder or spreadsheet called "licences" costs nothing and is frequently the thing that saves a channel from a takedown.

Checklist before you ship

  • Licence type matches the use: standard for your own content, extended for merchandise or multi-product use.
  • Paid advertising use is explicitly permitted if you run ads.
  • Any people or property have appropriate releases for commercial use.
  • Music bundled with the clip is cleared for monetised, cross-platform use.
  • You are not reselling the raw file, only incorporating it into an end product.
  • Receipt and licence terms are saved and linked from your project notes.
  • Credit requirements from the library are met in your description.

Summary

Stock media removes copyright risk only when you buy the right licence for the right job. Standard versus extended versus editorial is the decision that matters, releases cover the likeness problem, and keeping the licence file is what turns a claim into a five-minute dispute.
