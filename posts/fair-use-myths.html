8 Fair Use Myths That Get Creators Claimed

These eight beliefs are the reason most claimed channels believed they were safe. Each one is wrong for a specific, identifiable reason — and each has a fix that costs you nothing.

7 min read Myth-busting

Fair use myths are dangerous not because they are widely believed by anyone with legal training, but because they are widely believed by creators. Believing you are covered when you are not is what turns a small mistake into a strike.

Myth 1: "It's only a few seconds"

Why it fails: There is no seconds rule. Length is one input to factor three, alongside whether you took the most recognisable part. Ten seconds of a chorus or a signature shot can be the entire value of the original.

Fix: Ask what your purpose requires, then cut to that length. Ten seconds that are necessary and transformative are strong; thirty seconds because you liked the song are not.

Myth 2: "I credited the artist"

Why it fails: Attribution is not a legal defence and does not defeat a Content ID claim or a DMCA notice. Fair use does not require credit; licensing does.

Fix: Credit anyway — it is good practice and it satisfies licence terms — but never treat it as your legal protection.

Myth 3: "I'm not monetising it"

Why it fails: Non-commercial use only helps in the balancing test. It does not make an otherwise infringing use lawful. Platforms and rights holders still pursue unmonetised infringement, and your channel can be monetised later — retroactively making an old video a problem.

Fix: Judge the use on its own merits. If it would be infringing monetised, it is not safe unmonetised.

Myth 4: "I added my own commentary"

Why it fails: Commentary on top of a clip is not automatically transformative. If the clip is the point and the commentary is an intro, courts and platforms can reasonably see a substitutive work wearing commentary as a hat.

Fix: Make the commentary structural, not decorative. Refer to specific moments throughout, argue, compare, explain. Cut anything your analysis does not need.

Myth 5: "It's a mashup / remix, so it's transformative"

Why it fails: Transformative is judged on purpose, not on the label. Many mashups and "remixes" are primarily entertainment derived from the original works. Where the original is recognisable and your work adds no new meaning, the label does not help.

Fix: Test it honestly: does my version comment on, critique, or reinterpret the source, or does it just consume it?

Myth 6: "My channel is a fair use channel"

Why it fails: Some creators invoke a per-channel "fair use" flag in Content ID as if it were a licence. It is a dispute preference at best — not immunity — and it applies per video, not per channel.

Fix: Treat fair use as an argument you make in a dispute form, with your reasoning written down, per video.

Myth 7: "It's fair use because it's a reaction / I'm adding value"

Why it fails: The reaction genre is where the weakest fair use claims live. If your video could be deleted with no meaningful loss — because it is entirely a clip with an overlay — that is the definition of market substitution.

Fix: Make the reaction substantive, or reframe: analyse rather than react, use your own footage, or leave the clip out entirely.

Myth 8: "Unlisted / private means nobody saw it"

Why it fails: Unlisted videos are shared by link and are publicly accessible. Fair use does not require a monetised audience — the factors do not test "did anyone actually watch it". Sharing an unlisted video widely is publishing.

Fix: Judge the use by its purpose and market effect, not by the visibility setting.

The pattern behind all eight

Every myth on this list is a misunderstanding about fair use being a rule rather than a reasoned judgement. Fair use asks a question — is this new, necessary, and non-substitutive expression in the public interest? — and expects an argument. If your plan is a sentence explaining why you are exempt, you do not have a fair use plan; you have a hope.

What to do instead

  • Decide the purpose first: criticism, review, parody, teaching — or none of those, in which case licence.
  • Cut to the minimum necessary for your purpose.
  • Make your own contribution structural, not decorative.
  • Credit the source (good practice) without treating it as permission.
  • Write three sentences justifying each excerpt, before you upload.
  • Have a licensed fallback ready — a stock track you can drop in if a dispute goes badly.
  • Read the full guide to Fair Use Basics before relying on any of this.
