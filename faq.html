FAQ

The questions creators ask us most

Answers without hedging where the answer is clear, and honest acknowledgement where it genuinely depends on your situation.

Using other people's work

Can I use it at all?

Can I use a song if it's only 5 or 10 seconds?

There is no universal seconds rule, so the honest answer is: sometimes yes, and it depends on how you use it. Length is one input to a broader analysis — what the excerpt is for, whether your video adds its own meaning, whether you monetise, and whether it substitutes for hearing or buying the track itself. A 10-second excerpt serving a clear critical point in a review is in a strong position. A 10-second excerpt because it is the bit everyone recognises, in a video that would work fine without it, is not. See Fair Use Basics.

Does crediting the artist make it legal?

No. Credit is a courtesy, and it satisfies licence terms — but it does not create permission and it does not defeat a claim or a takedown. Rights holders routinely receive notices from videos that credit them perfectly. Credit anyway, because it is right and costs nothing; just never treat it as your legal protection. More on attribution.

Can I use music from another creator's video?

Almost certainly not, unless that creator holds the rights and has licensed it to you. When someone downloads a track and re-uploads it, they are the ones infringing — and that does not transfer any right to you. You would need your own licence from the rights holder. This is one of the most common ways new creators get claimed within weeks of starting.

What does "royalty-free" actually mean?

That the copyright is still very much alive, and the creator has waived ongoing royalties in exchange for a fee or subscription. The use is still governed by the licence — which may restrict commercial use, platforms, duration, credit, or redistribution. "Copyright-free" is a marketing term with no fixed legal meaning and usually means the same thing. Full explanation.

Is old music automatically free to use?

Only the composition, eventually. The sound recording is a separate copyright with its own term, so a 1910 melody in a 1990 recording still requires a licence for the recording. The same applies to restored or colourised films — a restoration with new music or intertitles is a new copyrighted work. Check both layers.

Platforms and claims

What happens when something lands

Is a Content ID claim a copyright strike?

No. A claim is an automated fingerprint match and a monetisation rule between the platform and the rights holder — not an accusation of infringement. It usually costs you the ad revenue for that video, and in some regions it blocks playback. It does not by itself count toward a strike. It can escalate if the rights holder escalates, and repeated claims on a channel attract attention. See Content ID Claims.

How long do I have to dispute a claim?

On YouTube, the standard window to dispute a Content ID claim is 30 days from the claim date. DMCA counter-notices have their own deadlines set by law and vary by country, and they are shorter and less forgiving. Never assume you have time — treat any copyright notice as same-day work.

How many copyright strikes before a channel is closed?

Three active copyright strikes is the widely implemented model: warning, then further restrictions, then termination. First strikes typically expire after around 90 days. Rights holders can also file fraudulent/DMCA notices against channels with a pattern of infringement, which can act much faster. Your channel dashboard is the authority on your own channel — check it rather than trusting any general rule, including this one.

My video got a claim but I had permission. What now?

Dispute it with evidence attached: the licence PDF, the receipt, or the permission email thread with dates. Say what you used, who granted permission, when, for which platforms and term. Save a copy of everything you submit. Most claims are released when the paperwork is there. Do not delete the video first — it is your evidence.

A music library track got claimed. Isn't that the library's problem?

It usually is theirs, but you still have to act. Some free libraries host tracks uploaded by people who did not own the rights, and artists sometimes enrol tracks in Content ID after release. Dispute it with your licence, and if the licence does not help, replace the track with something reliably cleared and keep a licensed fallback library for exactly this situation.

Money and growth

Commercial reality

Is unmonetised content automatically safe?

No. Non-commercial use helps in the fair use balance but does not make infringement lawful. And it is fragile: you may turn monetisation on later, take a sponsorship, or put the video behind a paid membership, and suddenly the same use is commercial and outside what you had cleared. Judge the use on its merits, not on your current revenue settings.

I use a track marked "non-commercial" in a monetised video. How bad is it?

It is a licence breach, and it is one of the most common problems in creator licensing. Often the fix is trivial: swap the track, or upgrade the licence, sometimes for a small fee. Do it before anyone complains — a proactive fix is far cheaper than a claim.

Can I license stock footage and then sell it in a template?

Not usually with a standard licence — most prohibit redistributing the asset or using it in a product for sale, and your buyers would not have rights to the underlying footage either. Buy a template or extended licence specifically, and check the terms say what you need. Stock licences explained.

The awkward one

When you have already messed up

I have been using the same track for two years. Now what?

Work out what you actually have. If it is properly licensed, keep the paperwork and make sure the licence covers current platforms and your current commercial status. If it is a "royalty-free" subscription, check it is still active. If you cannot identify a licence at all, replace the track in your most-viewed videos first and in the rest as time allows. Then fix the process so it does not recur. Use the checklist.

I got a DMCA strike I think was wrong. Can I fight it?

Yes — by filing a counter-notice, which is a sworn statement that the material was removed through mistake or misidentification. It has legal consequences, so only file one when you are confident it is accurate. Many disputes are resolved far more easily by contacting the notifier directly with your licence evidence. Read DMCA Takedowns & Strikes first, and get advice if there is money or a channel at stake.

Someone threatened me over copyright. What should I do?

Stay factual and stay on the platform's process. Do not retaliate, do not threaten back, do not coordinate pile-ons — platforms remove channels for that behaviour faster than for copyright disputes. Gather your licence evidence, respond through the official dispute or counter-notice channels, and if it escalates, get legal advice. Most rights holders would rather grant a licence than pursue a claim.

Someone used my song or my footage without permission. What can I do?

Start with evidence: dated originals, project files, first publication dates, registration records. Then use the platform's reporting tools for that specific infringement — do not file mass or misleading reports, as that is itself actionable. For commercial work, a takedown notice or a cease-and-desist letter from a lawyer is often the fastest route. And consider enrolling your work in Content ID so it is enforced automatically in the first place.
