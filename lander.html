Glossary

Every term a platform will use on you

Copyright jargon is mostly a handful of precise words used badly. Here they are, in plain English, grouped by what you are trying to do — licence something, argue something, or defend against something.

Foundations

Core concepts

Copyright

The bundle of exclusive rights in an original work — to reproduce, distribute, adapt, perform and display it. It exists automatically on creation, without registration or a copyright symbol.

Public domain

Works whose copyright has expired, was never renewed, or was dedicated to the public. Free to use with no conditions. Check the recording separately — the composition and the master can be in different states.

Licence / license

Permission from the copyright owner to do specific things with a work, on specific terms. It grants rights; it never transfers ownership.

Exclusive licence

A licence where the owner grants you permission and agrees not to license anyone else in the same territory, media or period. Often costs more and limits what else you can use.

Non-exclusive licence

The normal case: you may use the work, and so may others, within the stated scope. Most creator licences are non-exclusive.

Work made for hire

Work created by an employee within the scope of their job, where the employer is the copyright owner. Freelance commissions are usually not work for hire unless the contract says so.

Assignment

A written transfer of copyright ownership. If you commissioned a freelancer and did not get an assignment, you may not own the result — check the contract.

Moral rights

Rights in many countries to be named as the author and to object to distortion or derogatory treatment of your work. Usually not blocked by a fair commercial licence.

Licensing music

Music rights

Composition

The underlying song — melody, lyrics, chord progression. Written by a songwriter, owned by that writer or their publisher.

Master recording

The specific audio of a particular performance. A separate copyright from the composition, usually owned by the artist, producer or label.

Sync licence

Permission to use a copyrighted work in synchronisation with moving images — synchronising music to a video. Granted by whoever controls the rights in the underlying work.

Master licence

Permission to use the specific sound recording. This is what stops you being matched on a track even when your sync rights are fine.

Mechanical licence

Permission to reproduce the composition — physical copies, downloads, and in some cases streaming. For cover songs this is the main right you need, not sync.

Performance / public performance

The right to play a work publicly in a public space. Usually handled for you by your platform, broadcaster or a collecting society.

Royalty-free

A marketing term: pay once or subscribe, owe no ongoing royalties. The copyright is alive and the use is limited by the licence. See the guide.

Copyright-free

Another marketing term with no fixed legal meaning. Almost always means the same as royalty-free. Treat it as such until the licence says otherwise.

Blanket licence

A licence covering a large catalogue for a defined use, arranged between a platform and a rights holder — the usual reason platforms can host music at all.

Collecting society / PRO

An organisation that collects performance royalties on behalf of songwriters and performers. Registering your own songs with one is how you get paid for other people using them.

Exceptions

When you may not need permission

Fair use

A US legal defence permitting certain uses without permission — criticism, comment, news, teaching, scholarship, research. Judged on four factors, with purpose carrying the most weight. A defence to be argued, not a permission to assume.

Fair dealing

The equivalent concept in many other countries, using a closed list of permitted purposes. Often narrower than US fair use — check the rules where you are publishing.

Transformative use

A use that adds a new purpose, meaning, message or perspective, rather than substituting for the original. The central idea in a fair use argument — and the one most often asserted without substance.

Substitutive use

A use that could replace the original — someone could watch your version instead of buying or watching theirs. The opposite of transformative, and fatal to a fair use claim.

Parody

A work that comments on or ridicules an existing work. A recognised fair use purpose — but it must actually target the original, not borrow its audience.

Quotation / quotation right

Some jurisdictions allow short excerpts for purposes such as criticism or review, on condition the use is proportionate and the source acknowledged. Narrow, and not a licence for entertainment.

Creative Commons (CC)

A family of public licences letting creators pre-authorise reuse under stated conditions — BY (credit), SA (share alike), NC (non-commercial), ND (no derivatives). Read the specific licence on the specific work.

Attribution

Crediting the creator. Required by many licences and good practice always — but it is not a licence and does not create fair use.

Platform enforcement

Claims, notices and strikes

Content ID

A fingerprinting system that matches uploaded audio and video against registered works. It produces claims — monetisation or blocking decisions made by the rights holder, not legal findings.

Content ID claim

An automated match on a single video. Typical effects: the rights holder takes revenue, blocks the video in some territories, or does nothing visible. Disputable with a licence or a fair use argument.

Reference track

A recording registered in a fingerprinting system so others using it can be matched. Being on the reference list means a match is possible — not certain. Useful to check before you rely on a track.

DMCA

The US Digital Millennium Copyright Act. Also the informal name for a takedown notice sent to a platform. It creates a notice-and-takedown process: platforms remove valid notices quickly, before the merits are settled.

Takedown notice

A formal notice to a platform asserting that your video infringes copyright. It is a legal statement, typically sworn, and usually results in removal or restriction.

Copyright strike

The consequence of an upheld takedown on most major platforms. Three active strikes generally terminates a channel; the first typically expires after around 90 days. Check your own dashboard for the current rule.

Counter-notice

Your sworn response to a takedown, stating in good faith that the material was removed through mistake or misidentification. If the rights holder does not sue within the statutory window, the material is restored.

Fair, Use or Copyright abuse report

A platform's abuse channel for rights holders, used to report repeat or fraudulent notices. Available when you believe a claimant is acting in bad faith.

Fraudulent / DMCA notice

A notice filed against a channel with a pattern of infringement, designed to terminate the channel faster. Being on the receiving end usually means there is a genuine repeat problem, not one isolated video.

Channel strike / policy strike

Different from a copyright strike: this is for violating platform community guidelines. Distinct counters, distinct consequences — do not conflate them.

Stock and footage

Production vocabulary

Model release

A signed permission from a recognisable person allowing commercial use of their likeness in a specific production. Without one, commercial use of that footage can be risky even if it is properly licensed.

Property release

Permission from the owner of a private building, artwork or branded item visible in a shot, allowing commercial use.

Editorial use only

A restriction meaning the asset may illustrate news, education or commentary, but not promotional or commercial content — and never in a way implying endorsement.

Standard vs extended licence

Standard covers your own end products; extended covers merchandise, multiple products and often advertising. Getting this wrong is the standard-licence resale trap: you cannot sell a template built on a standard-licensed clip.

Perpetual licence

Permission that does not expire. Contrast with a subscription licence, which ends if you stop paying — a real risk for libraries you rely on across a back catalogue.

Archive footage

Historic or documentary footage licensed from archives. Status varies enormously by archive and clip, so the specific licence matters more than the label.
